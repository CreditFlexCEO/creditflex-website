Class 5: Protecting Your Credit

Beginner level • Approximately 15 minutes • Free educational course

Learn to recognize suspicious activity, protect your information, and respond if your identity is misused.

Lesson 1: Recognize identity theft

Identity theft occurs when someone uses your personal information without permission. Warning signs can include accounts you did not open, unfamiliar credit inquiries, unexpected bills, or collection notices for debts you do not recognize. Not every unfamiliar entry proves fraud; investigate it promptly.

Lesson 2: Credit freezes

A security freeze generally restricts access to your credit report for opening new credit accounts. Freezing and unfreezing your credit is free under federal law, and a freeze does not lower your credit score. You generally need to request a freeze separately from Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. A freeze does not prevent all forms of identity theft or block existing creditors from accessing information where permitted.

Visit the bureaus directly: Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion.

Lesson 3: Fraud alerts

A fraud alert tells businesses to take extra steps to verify your identity before extending new credit. You can request an initial fraud alert from one of the three nationwide credit bureaus; that bureau must notify the other two. Initial fraud alerts generally last one year. Eligible identity theft victims may qualify for an extended fraud alert lasting seven years. Fraud alerts and credit freezes are different protections.

Lesson 4: Monitoring and everyday safety

  • Review your credit reports for unfamiliar accounts and inquiries.
  • Use unique passwords and multifactor authentication where available.
  • Be suspicious of unexpected requests for Social Security numbers, verification codes, or account passwords.
  • Contact financial institutions using numbers on official statements or websites, not suspicious messages.
  • Understand that monitoring alerts can help you spot changes but do not prevent every kind of fraud.

You can access free credit reports through AnnualCreditReport.com.

Lesson 5: What to do if your identity is stolen

Contact affected financial institutions promptly to secure accounts and dispute unauthorized transactions. Report identity theft and create a recovery plan at IdentityTheft.gov, operated by the Federal Trade Commission. Consider credit freezes or fraud alerts and dispute fraudulent credit-report entries. Keep records of calls, letters, and case numbers. If you receive legal papers, respond by the deadline and consider legal help.

This course provides general educational information, not individualized legal advice.
