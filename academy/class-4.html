Class 4: Understanding Debt and Collections

Beginner level • Approximately 15 minutes • Free educational course

Learn how to recognize collection notices, check a claimed debt, and respond thoughtfully.

Lesson 1: What happens when a debt goes to collections?

When an account is unpaid, the original creditor may collect it, hire a collection agency, or sell it to a debt buyer. A collection account may appear on a credit report, depending on the type of debt and reporting practices. Being contacted by a collector does not by itself prove that the debt or amount is correct.

Lesson 2: Understand validation information

Under federal debt-collection rules, covered debt collectors generally must provide validation information about a debt, often in an initial communication or within five days afterward. This typically includes the collector's name, the creditor, the amount claimed, and how to dispute the debt. Review it carefully against your records.

If you dispute in writing within the 30-day validation period, a covered collector generally must pause collection of the disputed debt until it sends verification or a copy of a judgment, as applicable. Keep copies and proof of delivery. Different rules can apply depending on who is collecting and the type of debt.

Lesson 3: Know your rights

The federal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act restricts certain abusive, deceptive, and unfair practices by covered third-party debt collectors. For example, collectors generally cannot threaten actions they cannot legally take or repeatedly call to harass you. Some state laws provide additional protections.

If you receive court papers, do not ignore them. Respond by the stated deadline and consider legal aid or an attorney. Disputing a debt with a collector does not replace responding to a lawsuit.

Lesson 4: Before paying or settling

Confirm the collector and the amount, review any records, and consider your budget. Ask for settlement terms in writing before paying. A settlement may leave a remaining balance forgiven or may have potential tax consequences depending on circumstances. Payment or acknowledgment of an older debt can have legal consequences under some state laws, including rules concerning time limits for lawsuits. Check your state's rules or seek legal advice before acting.

Never share sensitive banking or identity information with an unverified caller.

Lesson 5: Collections and credit reporting

Most negative credit reporting is subject to time limits; collection accounts generally may remain on reports for about seven years from the relevant original delinquency date. Paying a collection does not necessarily remove accurate reporting. Credit-scoring models may treat paid collections differently. Dispute specific reporting errors with supporting documentation, rather than assuming every collection entry is inaccurate.

Learn more from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's debt collection resources.
