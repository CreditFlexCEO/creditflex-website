Understanding your paycheck

Imagine you accept a job paying $20 an hour. You work 40 hours a week, so you calculate $800 a week. You might think you have $1,600 to spend every two weeks. But when payday comes, your bank deposit is smaller. What happened?

The difference is usually taxes and other paycheck deductions. Learning to read those numbers is the first step toward building a budget that really works.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the difference between gross income and net income.
  • Recognize common paycheck deductions.
  • Calculate take-home pay from a sample pay stub.
  • Plan a dependable monthly budget for different pay schedules.
  • Explain why overtime and irregular earnings need special care.

1. Gross pay versus net pay

Gross pay is everything you earned before any deductions. Net pay is the amount left after deductions — usually the amount deposited in your bank account. When planning routine bills, start with a conservative estimate of your net pay rather than gross wages.

Example: Alexis earns $19 per hour and works 80 hours every two weeks. Her gross pay is $1,520. If total taxes and benefits are $423.28, her net paycheck is $1,096.72. Those are very different amounts to budget with.

2. What is deducted from your paycheck?

Your pay stub can list federal and state income tax withholding, Social Security, Medicare, health insurance, and retirement contributions. Not every paycheck has every deduction. Your deductions depend on your employment, location, benefits, and tax situation.

Pay stub itemPlain-English meaning
Federal income taxMoney withheld toward your federal income taxes.
State/local income taxTax withholding where applicable.
Social SecurityGenerally 6.2% of covered employee wages, up to the applicable annual wage base.
MedicareGenerally 1.45% of covered employee wages, with additional tax possible at higher earnings.
Health insurance / retirementBenefit and savings deductions, when applicable.

Social Security and Medicare together are often called FICA. For the ordinary employee wage example in this class, 6.2% + 1.45% = 7.65%. Actual withholding can be more complicated, especially for high earners or special employment circumstances.

3. Let's read a sample pay stub together

Keisha works 40 hours each week at $19 per hour and is paid every two weeks. Her gross paycheck covers 80 hours. Here is her illustrative pay stub:

ItemAmount
Gross wages (80 × $19)$1,520.00
Federal income tax (example)−$138.00
State/local income tax (example)−$74.00
Social Security (6.2%)−$94.24
Medicare (1.45%)−$22.04
Health insurance−$65.00
Retirement contribution−$30.00
Net pay$1,096.72

Her deductions add up to $423.28. Subtract that from $1,520 to get the $1,096.72 she actually takes home. The withholding amounts above are illustrations, not personalized tax calculations.

4. Weekly, biweekly, and twice-monthly are different

Weekly: Usually 52 paychecks per year. Biweekly: Every two weeks, usually 26 paychecks per year. Semimonthly: Twice each month, usually 24 paychecks per year. Monthly: Usually 12 paychecks per year.

For a cautious routine budget, a person paid biweekly can plan around two paychecks a month. In a typical year, two months will have three paydays. These additional checks can help fund savings, irregular bills, or debt payments. Budgeting around two checks is a planning choice, not the same thing as average monthly income.

Keisha's budget: Her net biweekly paycheck is $1,096.72. Two regular checks provide a monthly budget baseline of $2,193.44. Her average monthly income over 26 checks is about $2,376.23, but most months will not contain that exact amount.

5. What about overtime and changing hours?

Some workers receive overtime, tips, or bonuses that change from paycheck to paycheck. If your hours vary, review recent deposits and choose a conservative amount to cover necessary bills. Extra earnings can help with savings and upcoming expenses, but depending on them for every fixed bill can become risky when work slows down.

6. Key vocabulary

  • Gross pay: What you earn before deductions.
  • Net pay: What remains after deductions.
  • Withholding: Money an employer sets aside for taxes.
  • FICA: Social Security and Medicare payroll taxes.
  • Biweekly: Paid every two weeks.
  • Semimonthly: Paid twice each month.
Lesson takeaway: A paycheck has two stories: what you earned and what you can actually use. Understanding the difference makes everyday financial planning more realistic.
