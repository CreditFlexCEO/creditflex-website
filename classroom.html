WELCOME TO CLASS

A better way to learn about money.

This isn't a math test and you don't need to share personal financial information. We're going to make sense of a situation almost everyone recognizes: getting paid, paying bills, and trying to make the money last.

What you will learn today

  • Recognize the difference between income you are promised and money you actually receive
  • Explain why deductions and changing income affect household decisions
  • Identify dependable money for regular bills

“The point is not to know every financial term. The point is to understand enough to make a better decision the next time money comes in.”

TODAY'S COMPLETE INTRODUCTORY LESSON

Let's make sense of your money.

Follow along at your own pace. Each short section teaches one idea before moving on.

PART 1 OF 4

The paycheck is not the whole story

A job offer might say $20 an hour, but your bank deposit is usually smaller. Gross pay is the amount you earn before deductions. Net pay is the money remaining after taxes and other deductions. It is the net amount that is available for groceries, rent, transport, and other bills.

This is why some adults feel caught off guard by their first check at a new job. They made commitments based on the larger number. A sound starting point is not to memorize formulas, but to look at what reliably reaches the household.

PART 2 OF 4

Read the story your pay stub tells

A pay stub often contains earnings, hours, federal and state income tax withholding, Social Security and Medicare withholding, and benefits such as insurance. Some people also have retirement contributions. These entries answer two questions: What did I earn? What was taken out?

You do not need to be an accountant. Check that the hours and pay rate look right, that deductions are understandable, and that the final take-home amount matches the deposit. When something seems wrong, ask payroll to explain it.

PART 3 OF 4

When income arrives matters

A person paid every two weeks usually receives 26 paychecks over a year. A person paid twice each month normally receives 24. That difference matters when deciding which bills are due before the next deposit.

A practical way to organize regular bills is to plan around two biweekly paychecks in a normal month. Some months include a third check. That extra payday is not free money: it can help cover an upcoming expense, savings, or a bill that has been difficult to catch up on.

PART 4 OF 4

What to do if your hours change

Workers with changing shifts, tips, or overtime may have very different deposits from month to month. If you promise all of your largest paycheck to fixed bills, a slow month can become stressful.

Review several recent deposits, identify a conservative amount you can usually count on, and build basic obligations around that number where possible. Extra earnings can be assigned a purpose after they arrive. If the dependable amount does not cover essentials, the lesson is not that you failed; it tells you which gap needs a practical solution.

REAL-LIFE EXAMPLE

Keisha's Friday paycheck

Keisha sees $1,520 in gross earnings on her two-week pay stub. Her deductions total $423.28, leaving $1,096.72 deposited. She does not need to calculate a complicated tax formula to understand the lesson: the smaller number, not the gross amount, is what she can assign to bills. She also learns to check the dates on her bills against her actual paydays.

Remember: Find the take-home amount on a real or sample pay stub, and look at when it arrives.
